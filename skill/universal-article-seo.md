# Universal Article & Guide SEO Optimization Skill

## Purpose

Use this skill for every new article, guide, explainer, comparison, tutorial, review, research page, buying guide, or other editorial content published on a content website.

The goal is not to "add keywords." The goal is to create the most useful, accurate, crawlable, understandable, and search-intent-aligned page possible while giving search engines clear signals about what the page is about.

This is a **universal framework**. Adapt the process to the topic and content type instead of forcing every page into the same template.

---

## Core Principles

1. **People first, search engines second.**
2. Match the page to the **actual search intent**, not merely a keyword.
3. Answer important questions early; do not bury the answer under filler.
4. Cover the topic according to **user needs**, not an arbitrary word count.
5. Use natural language and related concepts; never keyword-stuff.
6. Prefer first-party, primary, government, academic, official, and otherwise authoritative sources where appropriate.
7. Never invent facts, citations, testing, credentials, statistics, prices, dates, or personal experience.
8. Clearly distinguish facts from reports, estimates, calculations, predictions, and editorial analysis.
9. Add something genuinely useful beyond what competing pages already provide.
10. Keep important information current when the topic changes over time.
11. Build logical internal links and topical clusters rather than isolated pages.
12. Use structured data only when it accurately represents visible page content and is appropriate for the page.
13. Avoid creating multiple pages that target essentially the same intent.
14. SEO optimization must never make the page misleading, repetitive, awkward, or harder to read.

---

# 1. INPUT: Understand the Page Before Optimizing

Before writing or optimizing, identify:

- Main topic
- Primary user question/problem
- Likely search intent
- Target audience
- Country/region if relevant
- Content type
- Primary topic/query
- Secondary questions and subtopics
- Important entities, products, people, organizations, places, concepts, dates, and attributes
- Whether the topic is evergreen, seasonal, or time-sensitive
- Whether the topic requires unusually high trust or evidence
- What the site already publishes about this subject
- What unique value this page can provide

If information is missing, do not invent it. Mark assumptions and request clarification only when it materially affects the result.

---

# 2. Detect the Content Type

Determine which model best fits the page. More than one may apply.

Possible types:

- Evergreen guide
- News/current-events article
- Explainer
- How-to/tutorial
- Comparison
- Product review
- Buying guide
- Product/specification guide
- Research/reference page
- List/roundup
- FAQ/resource page
- Opinion/editorial analysis
- Finance/consumer guide
- Software/tool guide
- Local guide

The content type determines the ideal structure, freshness requirements, schema opportunities, evidence standards, and search-result presentation.

Never force a generic "10 sections + FAQ" template onto every article.

---

# 3. Search Intent Analysis

Classify the dominant intent:

- Informational
- Navigational
- Commercial investigation
- Transactional
- Local
- Mixed

Then answer:

- What does the searcher want immediately?
- What decision are they trying to make?
- What follow-up questions naturally occur?
- What information would make them stop searching?
- What would frustrate them about existing results?

## Intent rule

The page must satisfy the dominant intent before attempting to maximize secondary SEO opportunities.

Examples:

- "What is X?" → definition and explanation first.
- "How to X" → steps, prerequisites, warnings, and expected result.
- "X vs Y" → direct comparison early, then detailed differences.
- "Is X worth it?" → decision criteria, trade-offs, alternatives, and conclusion.
- "X price" → current pricing, region/currency, date, what is included, and caveats.
- "Best X" → selection criteria, meaningful comparisons, evidence, and clear recommendations.

---

# 4. SERP & Competitor Research

When research tools are available, inspect relevant search results before finalizing the page.

Study:

- Search intent
- Common page types
- Titles and H1s
- Recurring subtopics
- Featured snippets / answer formats
- Tables and comparison formats
- Freshness expectations
- Sources competitors rely on
- Missing information
- Repeated low-value content
- Opportunities to provide better evidence, clarity, calculations, visuals, tools, examples, or regional detail

Do **not** copy competitor wording, structure, claims, or conclusions merely because they rank.

The objective is:

> Understand the search landscape → satisfy the intent → improve the information and experience.

---

# 5. Keyword & Topic Strategy

Create a natural topic map instead of a keyword-density target.

Identify:

### Primary topic/query
The central subject or query the page should satisfy.

### Secondary queries
Natural variations and important related questions.

### Semantic concepts/entities
People, products, organizations, attributes, specifications, concepts, dates, locations, and terminology that genuinely belong to the topic.

### Long-tail questions
Specific questions users may ask before or after the main query.

## Rules

- Use the primary topic naturally in the title/H1 and relevant places.
- Use synonyms and related terms where they improve clarity.
- Do not repeat the same phrase unnaturally.
- Do not create awkward headings solely to place keywords.
- Do not prescribe a keyword density percentage.
- Do not create separate pages for trivial keyword variations when the same page can satisfy them.

---

# 6. Build the Information Architecture

Before drafting, create a logical hierarchy:

**H1 → major H2 sections → supporting H3 sections**

The hierarchy should follow the user's information journey.

A useful sequence is often:

1. Direct answer / quick context
2. Essential facts
3. Core explanation
4. Detailed sections
5. Comparisons / examples / calculations where useful
6. Trade-offs / limitations
7. Recommendation or conclusion when appropriate
8. Useful FAQs only if they add genuinely distinct value

This is a guideline, not a mandatory template.

Avoid:

- Very long introductions
- Empty sections
- Repeating the same conclusion multiple times
- H2s that exist only for keywords
- Excessive nesting
- FAQ sections that duplicate the article

---

# 7. Title & H1 Optimization

Create:

- One clear H1
- One SEO title
- A concise, accurate meta description

## Title rules

- Clearly communicate the topic.
- Match search intent.
- Put important terminology early when natural.
- Make the benefit or angle clear when useful.
- Avoid clickbait.
- Avoid unnecessary capitalization and keyword repetition.
- Do not promise something the article does not deliver.

The SEO title and H1 may be similar, but they do not need to be identical.

Do not blindly enforce a character-count rule. Optimize for clarity and likely search-result display.

---

# 8. Meta Description

Write a useful description that:

- Accurately summarizes the page
- Reflects the searcher's intent
- Communicates what the reader will get
- Uses important terminology naturally
- Avoids keyword stuffing
- Avoids misleading claims

Treat character counts as practical guidelines, not hard rules, because search-result display can vary.

---

# 9. URL / Slug

Recommend a URL that is:

- Short
- Descriptive
- Stable
- Lowercase
- Hyphen-separated
- Easy to understand
- Free of unnecessary parameters, dates, IDs, or keyword repetition

Example:

`/iphone-18-pro-guide/`

Prefer a durable URL for evergreen content. Do not add a date merely because the article was published that year.

Avoid changing established URLs without a real reason and an appropriate redirect strategy.

---

# 10. Above-the-Fold Optimization

The opening portion should quickly establish:

- What the page is about
- Who it is for
- The most important answer/facts
- Why the page is useful

Depending on intent, consider:

- Quick-answer box
- Key facts table
- Price/date summary
- Comparison snapshot
- Step summary
- Recommendation
- Definition
- Important warning or eligibility note

Do not create a generic TL;DR on every page if it adds no value.

---

# 11. Direct-Answer Optimization

When the query is question-based, answer the question directly before expanding.

Good formats include:

- One-paragraph definitions
- Short factual answers
- Numbered steps
- Bulleted lists
- Comparison tables
- Short calculations
- Clearly labeled pros/cons

Make answers independently understandable where possible.

Never distort an answer simply to target a featured snippet.

---

# 12. Content Quality & Original Value

Every page should have a clear reason to exist.

Identify its **unique value proposition** before publishing.

Possible sources of original value:

- Original research
- Primary-source synthesis
- Fresh reporting
- Calculations
- Regional pricing or policy analysis
- Side-by-side comparisons
- Clear methodology
- Expert-reviewed information when genuinely available
- Original examples
- Practical decision frameworks
- Useful visuals or tables
- Updated information
- Better organization of fragmented information

A page should not merely paraphrase ten other pages.

Do not claim "we tested" unless the site actually performed the test.

Do not claim expertise, firsthand experience, surveys, interviews, or original research that did not occur.

---

# 13. Evidence, Citations & Source Quality

For factual content, use appropriate evidence.

Preferred source hierarchy depends on the subject, but generally prioritize:

1. Official/first-party sources
2. Government/regulatory sources
3. Academic/research sources
4. Original datasets or documentation
5. Reputable primary reporting
6. High-quality secondary sources
7. Community sources when firsthand experiences are specifically relevant

Use the source that actually supports the claim.

Do not cite a source merely because it discusses the topic.

## Source-status labels

When useful, distinguish:

- **Official / confirmed**
- **Reported**
- **Rumored**
- **Estimated**
- **Calculated**
- **Observed**
- **Our analysis**
- **Opinion**

Never present a rumor, prediction, calculation, or inference as a confirmed fact.

---

# 14. Accuracy & Freshness System

Identify claims that can change:

- Prices
- Product availability
- Specifications
- Release dates
- Laws/regulations
- Benefits/eligibility
- Interest rates
- Statistics
- Software features
- Policies
- Rankings
- Company leadership
- Market data
- Travel information
- Local information

For time-sensitive claims:

- Verify them against current sources.
- Include a relevant "last updated" date when useful.
- Explain the applicable region/date when necessary.
- Recheck important claims during future updates.

Do not change an article merely to make the update date look fresh. Updates should represent meaningful review or improvement.

---

# 15. Readability & UX

Optimize for scanning without making the article shallow.

Use:

- Short-to-medium paragraphs
- Descriptive headings
- Bullets for grouped items
- Numbered steps for sequences
- Tables for true comparisons
- Bold text sparingly for important concepts
- Definitions for unfamiliar terms
- White space
- Clear transitions

Avoid:

- Walls of text
- Filler introductions
- Repetitive conclusions
- Excessive bolding
- Fake urgency
- Keyword-stuffed headings
- Unnecessary jargon

Write for the reader's knowledge level.

---

# 16. Tables & Structured Information

Use tables when the reader benefits from comparing attributes.

Good uses:

- Product specifications
- Prices
- Plans
- Features
- Eligibility criteria
- Timelines
- Pros/cons
- Regional differences

Rules:

- Every column must serve a purpose.
- Keep mobile readability in mind.
- Do not cram paragraphs into table cells.
- Verify every value.
- Include units and dates where relevant.
- Do not use tables merely because they "look SEO-friendly."

---

# 17. Images & Media SEO

Use media when it improves understanding, not merely to decorate the page.

For each important image:

- Make it relevant.
- Use a descriptive filename.
- Write accurate alt text when appropriate.
- Do not stuff keywords into alt text.
- Use appropriate dimensions.
- Compress efficiently.
- Prefer modern formats where supported.
- Provide meaningful captions when useful.

For decorative images, avoid pretending they convey information.

For charts/diagrams, ensure the important information is also accessible as text.

---

# 18. Image Performance

Check:

- Image dimensions match display needs.
- Large images are compressed.
- Below-the-fold images can use lazy loading.
- The main above-the-fold/LCP image is not unnecessarily lazy-loaded.
- Width/height or equivalent layout information reduces layout shift.
- Responsive image delivery is used where practical.

Do not sacrifice useful visuals just to reduce image count.

---

# 19. Internal Linking

Build a useful internal network.

Link to:

- Closely related guides
- Supporting explanations
- Comparisons
- Definitions/glossary pages
- Relevant category/topic hubs
- Deeper research
- Relevant products

Use descriptive anchor text.

Good:

`how iPhone trade-ins are valued`

Weak:

`click here`

Rules:

- Links must be genuinely useful.
- Do not force a fixed number of links.
- Avoid repeating the exact same destination excessively.
- Link important new pages from relevant existing pages.
- Keep the site architecture understandable to both users and crawlers.

---

# 20. Topic Clusters & Site Architecture

For important subjects, consider:

**Pillar/Hub → Supporting Guides → Comparisons/Deep Dives → Glossary/Reference → Relevant Products**

Create pages only when they provide distinct value and satisfy distinct intent.

Avoid:

- Dozens of thin pages targeting tiny keyword variations
- Near-duplicate guides
- Artificial topic clusters
- Orphan pages

When two pages target essentially the same intent, consider improving, merging, redirecting, or differentiating them.

---

# 21. Cannibalization Check

Before publishing, ask:

- Does the site already have a page satisfying this exact intent?
- Is the new page genuinely different?
- Could an existing page be expanded instead?
- Are two pages competing for the same primary topic?
- Should one page become the canonical comprehensive resource?

Do not split one useful topic into many thin pages just to create more URLs.

---

# 22. External Links

External links should help the reader verify or explore information.

Prefer authoritative sources relevant to the claim.

For commercial/affiliate content, link to official product or provider information when appropriate.

Avoid:

- Link farms
- Irrelevant references
- Unverified sources for important claims
- Excessive outbound links
- Invented citations

When linking to sources, make the destination clear to the reader.

---

# 23. Structured Data

Use structured data only when:

- The schema type genuinely applies.
- The marked information is present and accurate on the page.
- The implementation follows current search-engine guidelines.

Potentially relevant types include, depending on the page:

- Article
- BlogPosting
- NewsArticle
- BreadcrumbList
- Product
- Review
- SoftwareApplication
- Recipe
- Event
- ProfilePage

Do not add schema simply because it exists.

Never fabricate ratings, reviews, prices, authorship, dates, or other structured data.

---

# 24. Article Structured Data

For article-like content, evaluate whether Article/BlogPosting/NewsArticle structured data is appropriate.

Where applicable, accurately represent properties such as:

- Headline
- Author
- Image
- Date published
- Date modified
- Main entity/topic

The structured data must agree with the visible page.

Do not use NewsArticle merely to make an evergreen article appear like breaking news.

---

# 25. Breadcrumbs

Use a logical breadcrumb hierarchy when appropriate:

Home → Category → Topic → Article

Breadcrumbs should represent the site's actual navigation hierarchy.

If implemented with structured data, the markup must match the visible breadcrumb information.

---

# 26. Author & Trust Signals

Where appropriate, provide:

- Author name
- Author profile
- Relevant credentials or experience that genuinely exist
- Publication date
- Updated date
- Sources
- Methodology
- Editorial policy
- Corrections policy
- Contact/about information
- Affiliate disclosure where relevant
- Clear disclaimers where necessary

Do not manufacture authority signals.

Trust comes from evidence, transparency, accuracy, and accountability—not decorative badges.

---

# 27. High-Stakes / YMYL Topics

For health, finance, legal, safety, public benefits, and similarly consequential subjects, raise the evidence standard.

Prioritize:

- Official sources
- Government documentation
- Qualified professional review when genuinely available
- Primary research
- Clear dates and jurisdiction
- Important limitations
- Appropriate disclaimers

Never present general information as individualized professional advice.

Avoid unsupported certainty.

---

# 28. Affiliate & Commercial Content

If the article can generate revenue through affiliate links or products:

The page must still provide genuine informational value.

Include where appropriate:

- Selection criteria
- Who the product is for
- Who should avoid it
- Alternatives
- Trade-offs
- Important specifications
- Price context
- Evidence supporting recommendations
- Affiliate disclosure

Never invent hands-on testing or personal experience.

Do not recommend the highest-paying option merely because it pays more.

If a recommendation is based on an editorial framework rather than firsthand testing, say so when relevant.

---

# 29. FAQ Strategy

Use FAQs only when they add useful, distinct information.

Good FAQ candidates:

- Real follow-up questions
- Important edge cases
- Clarifications not naturally handled elsewhere
- Region/date-specific questions
- Short factual questions with clear answers

Avoid:

- Repeating entire sections
- Creating dozens of near-identical questions
- Writing FAQs solely for schema
- Fabricating questions that users would not reasonably ask

If a question deserves a full section, make it a section instead of hiding it in an FAQ.

---

# 30. Featured Snippet & Search-Result Formatting

Make useful information easy to extract:

- Concise definitions
- Direct answers
- Ordered steps
- Bulleted lists
- Comparison tables
- Clearly labeled values

But never manipulate wording to produce a misleading snippet.

Search appearance is a consequence of good information architecture, not the sole goal of the page.

---

# 31. Technical SEO Checklist

For every published page, evaluate:

- Crawlable HTML
- Indexability
- Correct canonical URL
- HTTPS
- Mobile usability
- Responsive layout
- Fast page rendering
- Good Core Web Vitals where practical
- Stable layout
- Accessible typography
- Logical heading hierarchy
- Descriptive links
- Working internal/external links
- Image optimization
- Sitemap inclusion where appropriate
- Robots directives are intentional
- No accidental `noindex`
- No accidental canonical to another page
- Correct status code
- No unnecessary duplicate URL versions
- Open Graph metadata
- Social sharing image
- Relevant X/Twitter metadata where applicable

Do not block useful content behind inaccessible client-side behavior if search engines/users need the content.

---

# 32. Social / Sharing Metadata

Where relevant, configure:

- Open Graph title
- Open Graph description
- Open Graph image
- Canonical URL
- Social card metadata

Social metadata is primarily for presentation and sharing, but it should remain consistent with the page's actual topic and title.

---

# 33. Accessibility

SEO and accessibility overlap in important ways.

Check:

- Semantic HTML
- Meaningful headings
- Descriptive link text
- Accurate alt text
- Keyboard usability
- Sufficient text readability
- Form labels where relevant
- Captions/transcripts for meaningful media where appropriate
- No information conveyed only through color

Do not use accessibility attributes merely as SEO tricks.

---

# 34. Content Length

Never set a universal word-count target.

Determine sufficient length by asking:

> Can the reader accomplish what they came here to accomplish without needing another basic article?

A 700-word page can beat a 3,000-word page when the intent is simple.

A 4,000-word guide can be appropriate when the topic genuinely requires it.

Remove filler even if the page becomes shorter.

---

# 35. Content Completeness

Measure completeness by **important user needs**, not by word count.

Create a coverage map:

- Main question
- Essential facts
- Important subquestions
- Decision criteria
- Edge cases
- Risks/limitations
- Alternatives
- Examples
- Regional differences
- Current information
- Sources

Only include sections that help the reader.

---

# 36. Freshness & Update Strategy

For every article, classify:

- Evergreen
- Periodically changing
- Frequently changing
- Breaking/current

Then define what should trigger an update.

Examples:

- Product article → new model/spec/price/release
- Finance guide → law/rate/benefit change
- Software guide → major UI/API/version change
- Travel guide → opening/closing/rule/price change
- Evergreen explainer → update only when important evidence or understanding changes

Do not update dates without reviewing the content.

---

# 37. Content Maintenance Metadata

Where useful internally, record:

- Primary topic
- Content type
- Original publication date
- Last substantive review date
- Primary sources
- Time-sensitive claims
- Next review trigger
- Owner/editor
- Related pages

This helps maintain a large content library over time.

---

# 38. AI-Assisted Content Rules

AI may assist with:

- Research organization
- Outlining
- Drafting
- Rewriting
- Classification
- Gap analysis
- Metadata suggestions
- Internal-link suggestions
- Quality checks

But the final content must be:

- Fact-checked
- Original in value
- Honest about experience and methodology
- Free of fabricated citations
- Free of invented quotes
- Free of invented testing
- Edited for clarity and accuracy

Do not mass-produce pages that provide little or no value merely because AI can generate them quickly.

---

# 39. Search Intent vs. SEO Template Rule

**Intent wins over templates.**

If a standard SEO recommendation conflicts with what users actually need, prioritize the user.

Examples:

- Do not add an FAQ just because SEO templates commonly contain FAQs.
- Do not add 2,000 words because competitors have 2,000 words.
- Do not add five H2s when two sections answer the question better.
- Do not add a keyword to every heading.
- Do not create a comparison table when there is nothing meaningful to compare.

# 41. Required Output When Asked to SEO-Optimize an Article

When given an existing article/draft and asked to optimize it, do **not automatically rewrite the entire article** unless explicitly requested.

Return the following sections:

## A. SEO Diagnosis
- Current intent
- Content type
- Primary topic/query
- Secondary topics
- Audience
- Main strengths
- Main weaknesses
- Biggest SEO opportunities

## B. Search Strategy
- Recommended search intent
- Topic/entity map
- Important questions to cover
- Competitor/SERP observations when research is available
- Content differentiation opportunity

## C. Metadata
- Recommended SEO title
- Recommended H1
- Recommended meta description
- Recommended URL slug

## D. Content Structure
- Recommended section hierarchy
- Sections to add
- Sections to remove/merge
- Ordering changes

## E. Content Improvements
For each major issue:
- Problem
- Why it matters
- Exact recommended change

## F. Internal Linking
Suggest relevant existing or planned pages and useful anchor concepts. Never invent pages that do not exist unless explicitly labeling them as future content ideas.

## G. External Evidence
Identify claims that need stronger or more current sources.

## H. Structured Data
Recommend only schema types that genuinely apply.

## I. Technical SEO
List implementation checks relevant to the page.

## J. Freshness & Maintenance
Identify time-sensitive claims and what should trigger future updates.

## K. Final Scorecard
Give a practical assessment across:

- Intent
- Content quality
- Coverage
- Original value
- On-page SEO
- Internal linking
- Trust/evidence
- Technical SEO
- Freshness
- UX/readability

Do not invent numerical precision. Scores should be useful diagnostics, not fake measurements.

# 45. Universal Decision Framework

For every page, ask these questions in order:

### 1. What does the user want?
Identify the dominant intent.

### 2. What is the best page type?
Choose the format that naturally satisfies that intent.

### 3. What must the user know?
Build the minimum complete information set.

### 4. What evidence is required?
Match source quality to claim importance.

### 5. What is changing?
Identify freshness-sensitive information.

### 6. What makes this page better?
Define the unique value.

### 7. How should the page be structured?
Make the information easy to navigate and scan.

### 8. How should search engines understand it?
Use clear titles, headings, links, metadata, semantic content, and appropriate structured data.

### 9. How does it fit the site?
Add logical internal links and avoid cannibalization.

### 10. Is it actually useful?
Perform a final human review.

---

# 46. Quality Standard

A page is ready when it is:

**Useful + Accurate + Intent-aligned + Original in value + Well-structured + Well-sourced + Crawlable + Maintainable**

SEO is successful when it helps the right people discover a page that genuinely deserves their attention.

Do not optimize for search engines at the expense of the reader.
